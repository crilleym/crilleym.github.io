Can an origami structure absorb energy in space?

Launch and space systems reward structures that use less mass and volume without giving up predictable mechanical performance. My research investigates Kresling origami tubes: thin-walled structures that convert axial compression into controlled rotation, creating a tunable collapse response for future energy-absorbing and deployable systems.

From geometry to a predictive model

Uniform tubes usually deform primarily along their axis. A Kresling unit behaves differently: its angled facets and fold lines couple compression with torsion. We varied three geometric inputs, extracted peak force, peak rotation, and initial stiffness from the simulation curves, then used predictive Kriging with a Gaussian correlation and second-order polynomial regression to estimate responses throughout the design space.

What the simulations predicted—and the poster experiments supported

FEA + Kriging study

  • Increasing wall thickness increased initial stiffness.
  • Increasing wall thickness increased peak force.
  • Rotation remained comparatively insensitive to thickness.
  • The 36 simulations enabled prediction between tested parameter combinations.

Poster + physical study

  • Axial compression produced a force peak, load drop, and cross-sectional rotation.
  • Maximum reaction force increased with wall thickness.
  • Maximum rotation increased with unit height.
  • The 27 CAD models supported the search for an energy-absorption optimum.
Combined conclusion: the studies agree that wall thickness is the dominant lever for load capacity, while height is a stronger lever for rotation. That separation is valuable: it suggests force response and twisting motion can be tuned somewhat independently, creating a pathway toward lightweight structures designed for a specific peak load, collapse stroke, and energy-absorption target.

System architecture

The ventilation system used a blower to pull air across a branching network of molded cushion channels. Performance depended on the entire load path and airflow path—not only the channel geometry—so I studied the blower and motor location, suspension hooks, foam openings, gasket interfaces, and how the cushion deformed under an occupant.

Root cause: local collapse at the H-point

The first prototype performed well unoccupied but lost airflow under occupant loading. Physical inspection showed that a foam-channel opening near the H-point was being compressed against the suspension mat and gasket. Instead of pulling air across the entire channel set, the system encountered a local restriction directly above the blower. This narrowed the root cause from “insufficient blower performance” to a geometric and structural interaction under load.

Material-science study: foam skin and sealer

I ran a separate component-level study to determine whether prototype foam accurately represented molded production foam. Three samples isolated surface condition as the variable: skived foam without skin, skived foam with latex sealer, and molded foam with its natural skin.

Skived foam tracked molded-foam airflow behavior more closely than expected. Adding latex sealer raised pressure and did not accurately reproduce the molded skin, so the sealed sample was not a valid production surrogate.
Concept selection after root cause analysis

Once the restriction mechanism was understood, I used a Pugh chart to compare ten targeted countermeasures against airflow performance, cost, tooling impact, part manufacturability, H-point comfort, and timing. The highest-ranked root-cause countermeasure was to deepen the channel locally by 7 mm and align the foam opening, carrier hole, and foam gasket. This evidence-based comparison narrowed the path forward before the final production-focused redesign removed a component.

Pick-and-Place Robot
Manufacturing & Design I — Team Lead
Led the design, analysis, fabrication, and iteration of a mobile pick-and-place robot with a hook arm and lasso-and-winch mechanism.
▸ See the full design process Hide full design process
The challenge

The brief was a mobile robot competing in a Wild-West-themed course: open a saloon door, collect lassos, drive up a mountain, and place lassos on a horse — split across four robots (RMP1–4) working the course together. As team lead, I owned the strategy for our robot (RMP3), the concept selection, the analysis behind the mechanisms, and the build.

Strategy & requirements

With four robots on the course at once, RMP3's role came down to sequencing: let the other robots start their own objectives immediately, then make RMP3 the team's most reliable lasso-scorer. The selected strategy was:

  • Let RMP1 and RMP4 open the saloon door themselves and get moving right away
  • Collect the prairie lassos
  • Drive up the mountain and place lassos on the horse
  • Meet RMP2 and place any lassos it didn't get to
  • Park in the mountain's first-tier spot
  • If time remains, help with the track objective

We picked this order because lasso placement scored more consistently than the team's other soft-object task (hanging hats), so it made sense to have one robot specialize in it; acting as a backup lasso-placer for RMP2 also added robustness if another robot ran into delays, and the optional track work kept RMP3 productive if lassos were finished early.

That strategy set three functional requirements, each with a target and an allowable margin of error:

  • Grab the door handle within 20 s (–4 s margin) — early enough that the other RMPs have time to get through the door; the margin accounts for realistic variability like parking position and motor start lag.
  • Open the door to at least 60° (–8° margin) — comfortably enough for a 10"×10" robot to clear the doorway (27.4·sin θ gives roughly 21.6" at 52° and 23.8" at 60°, both well past the 10" needed).
  • Place at least 3 lassos on the horse (–1 lasso margin) — allows for one missed attempt while still proving the mechanism reliably scores.
Concept selection — Pugh chart

Four early concepts were scored against those three requirements plus manufacturability and creativity, weighted by importance:

Cully #1 (baseline) — forklift-style up/down-and-pivot arm. Plausible for both the door and the lassos, and the pivoting motion gives a way to apply torque to the handle, but it likely relies on near-perfect arm placement, risks instability, and the forklift mechanism itself is difficult to manufacture.

Samaria #1 — vertical lift + in/out arm, front latch hook for the door (+3). Once hooked, the door pull is strong and reliable — the main tradeoff is that handle acquisition is alignment-sensitive, and the extending arm is harder to build and no more accurate at placing lassos than the baseline.

Leo #2 — telescoping arm with horizontal jaws (–5). Simple and creative to build, but the jaws are prone to slipping off the handle unless the pull lines up almost exactly tangent to the door's swing, and placing lassos accurately with it proved difficult.

My concept #1 — double arm with lasso storage, hook/claw for the door, omni wheels (+5). A dedicated hook made handle acquisition strong and the build straightforward, but door-opening stability depended on traction and geometry rather than being a clear win over baseline, and carrying lassos in a storage rack didn't by itself guarantee accurate placement.

My concept #1 and Samaria #1 scored highest, but neither fully satisfied every requirement on its own. The design that actually won out combined Leo's pulley arm with my hook arm: Leo's mechanism scored well on manufacturability and lasso handling, while my dedicated hook solved the door problem more reliably than a claw or latch. Re-scored as a hybrid, that combined design came out on top at +14 — a dedicated hook arm for the handle instead of a finicky claw, and a drivetrain-assisted pull once hooked, for a secure, forceful door-open.

Key trade studies

Three subsystem decisions needed real analysis before they went into CAD:

Drivetrain gear ratio (first-principles analysis). Getting up the ramp to reach the horse was itself a functional requirement, so I balanced gear ratio against motor torque and speed: too low a ratio risked the motor stalling and the robot rolling back down the ramp; too high made the climb needlessly slow. The analysis found the ratio that could make the climb without stalling, without giving up more speed elsewhere than necessary.

Hook throat length (empirical test). The hook arm carried our hardest requirement — open the door in under 20 seconds — so after sizing it analytically I tested the throat length empirically: too short and the hook slips off the handle mid-pull; too long and it can't maneuver into the handle in the first place. Testing found the length that maximized grip while still fitting the handle geometry cleanly.

Winch torque on the pulley arm (first-principles analysis). The pulley arm both retrieves and places lassos, so too little torque and it can't lift the arm or get a lasso over the horse; too much and fine positioning gets sloppy, which hurts placement accuracy. The analysis set the torque needed to lift and hold the arm steady without sacrificing the control needed for accurate placement.

Layout drafts

I sketched side- and top-view layouts to work out where the drivetrain, winch, and hook arm needed to sit relative to each other before moving into CAD, so the arm's swing and the winch's cable run wouldn't collide with the chassis or each other.

CAD & fabrication

The drafts became a full CAD assembly, which I used to finalize part geometry, check clearances, and hand off for fabrication and wiring.

Final result

The finished robot ran the course at competition using the hook arm and lasso-and-winch mechanism as designed.

Over-Wing Push Bar
MRacing Formula SAE — Suspension & Chassis Team
Collaborated on a 4-person team to design and manually machine a push bar mounted over the rear wing of a 420-lb Formula SAE car, replacing a previous design that was awkward to install and use. I owned the bucket/mount subassembly, the hand-calculated free-body analysis behind the mounting loads, several of the manufacturing drawings, and helped hand-machine the final parts.
▸ See the full design-to-final-fit breakdown Hide full design-to-final-fit breakdown
Problem & requirements

Our car needed a way for one person to push it from behind without leaning on the rear aero package — the previous push bar design was awkward to install and get onto the roll hoop, especially working from behind the car. We set out to replace it with a bar that mounted over the rear wing, bolted onto the roll hoop, and was simple enough for one person to install and use. I worked on a 4-person team, owning the bucket/mounting subassembly, the hand free-body analysis, part of the manufacturing drawing set, and hand-machining.

Free-body diagram & hand calcs

I modeled the push bar as a beam: the horizontal handle picks up the pushing load, and that load has to react through the two vertical legs into the roll-hoop mounting points. I sketched it out as a simply-supported/overhung beam and solved for the reaction forces at each mount (FR1, FR2) so I knew what the mounting brackets and their bolts actually had to carry — instead of just guessing at fastener sizes.

I also had to answer a more basic question: could one person actually hold the vertical-bar assembly up against the roll hoop while installing it, or would it need a second set of hands? I modeled the assembly in NX, assigned it 6061 aluminum, and pulled its mass — 2.41 lb. To sanity-check what a person can comfortably hold out at arm's length, I'd tried a similar hand-calculation the day before for a class problem (holding a weight on the end of a broomstick) and actually tested it by hand, which put the practical one-person limit around 4 lb. At 2.41 lb, the vertical-bar assembly cleared that with margin, so we knew installation wouldn't need a second person just to hold it steady.

Design review — what almost went wrong

Before anything got machined, our CAD review turned up a few things that would have been real problems on the car:

Bolt clearance: one of the through-bolts on the vertical-bar mount would have interfered with the roll hoop itself. We fixed it by moving to a fine-thread bolt tapped directly into the rectangular tube instead of a through-bolt, and shifted the bolt position higher to guarantee clearance and enough room to actually torque it down.

Tolerance stack-up: the vertical tube was constrained against rotating relative to the horizontal tube only by contacting faces — fine with perfect tolerances, but we don't get perfect tolerances. We added a second bolt to positively constrain that rotation instead of relying on a face fit.

Fire extinguisher mount: the original plan was to strap the extinguisher on with a velcro strip epoxied to a mounting plate. Partway through the build we scrapped that for a purchased extinguisher bracket — more reliable and one less bonded joint to trust in a safety-critical mount.

Fabrication & assembly

I built the bucket subassembly — the welded channel that the horizontal handle bar seats into — while teammates machined the gussets and bars. Everything was assigned and tracked (buckets, gussets, vertical bars, horizontal bars, fire extinguisher mount) with a shared drawing due-date, since four of us were machining different parts of the same assembly in parallel.

We welded only where the joint needed to be permanent — the handle bar to the horizontal bars, the extinguisher plate to the horizontal bars, and the bucket edges to the vertical bars — and bolted everything else so the bar stayed serviceable. The lower bucket holes were bolted only temporarily, to hold the bucket and vertical bars square to each other while we welded; those bolts came out once the welds were done.

Final fit & result

The finished bar bolted onto the roll hoop over the rear wing, and — per the whole point of the redesign — one person could actually get it on and off and use it to push the car by themselves.

Chassis Insert Test Assembly
MRacing Formula SAE — Suspension & Chassis Team
Designed chassis insert test assemblies in Siemens NX, validated structural performance with ANSYS FEA up to 331 MPa, and produced 20+ manufacturing drawings with full GD&T for the fixture.
▸ See the full design-to-fabrication breakdown Hide full design-to-fabrication breakdown
Goal

Design, test, and qualify carbon-fiber two-force members for MR25 — bonded-in aluminum inserts, similar in concept to the ones already used on our steering column, so the load path from a solid metal fitting into a hollow carbon tube would be well understood before we trusted it anywhere else on the car.

Design considerations & variables

Before sizing a single insert, I mapped out everything that could actually affect a bonded joint's real-world strength, so the test matrix wouldn't just be guesswork:

  • Bond area / bond length
  • Bond thickness — i.e. the depth of the groove cut into the insert
  • Epoxy type
  • Strength in tension vs. compression (bonded joints don't necessarily behave the same in both)
  • Fatigue life and long-term carbon aging
  • Rod-end fit and interface
  • DFMEA / PFMEA — design- and process-level failure modes to catch before they became real failures
  • Whether testing needed to be destructive to actually find the failure load
Benchmarking & key decisions

Rather than guess at a bond length, I checked in with RIT's FSAE team, who'd found that bond strength shows diminishing returns past about 1 inch — so we adopted a 1" bond length as our baseline instead of over-building the joint for no real strength gain. We also settled on aluminum inserts, consistent with the approach already proven on our steering column, rather than introducing a new insert material to validate from scratch.

Modeling & FEA

I modeled the test assembly in Siemens NX, then validated it with ANSYS FEA before committing to fabrication — checking that the fixture itself wouldn't yield or distort under the test loads and skew the results. That analysis showed peak stresses up to 331 MPa at the worst-case load locations, which we used to confirm margin against the material before signing off on the design.

Manufacturing drawings

From that validated model I produced more than 20 manufacturing drawings with full GD&T callouts covering every fixture component, so the shop could machine each part to the tolerances the test setup actually needed — alignment and hole tolerances matter a lot more on a test fixture than on a one-off bracket, since the fixture itself has to introduce as little error as possible into the measurement.

Fabrication

The fixture components and insert specimens — made at different bonding lengths so we could isolate bond length as a variable — were machined from those drawings and assembled for testing.

Testing

Specimens are pull/compression-tested on an Instron-type load frame, run to failure so the destructive test actually reveals the true failure load and mode rather than just an assumed value — tested in both tension and compression, since carbon-to-metal bonded joints can fail differently depending on load direction.
